## 1. The Gallio thing

The one date in Paul's life that sits on a stone, not only on a Bible verse.

### The scene (Acts 18:1–17)

Paul is in Corinth about eighteen months (18:11). He stays with Aquila and Priscilla, "lately come from Italy" because Claudius had expelled Jews from Rome (18:2). Jews haul him to the **bema** and accuse him of persuading people to worship God contrary to the law. The judge is **Gallio, proconsul of Achaia**. Gallio will not take the case. He treats it as an in-house Jewish argument, drives them from the bench, and looks away while Sosthenes is beaten. Paul stays a while, then sails.

One official. One city. One hearing. That is the whole New Testament appearance.

### Who Gallio was

**Lucius Junius Gallio Annaeanus**, brother of the philosopher **Seneca the Younger**, adopted into the Gallio family. Achaia was a senatorial province (Athens, Corinth, Delphi). The governor was a **proconsul**, normally about one year, often arriving in early summer. Name a one-year official correctly and you have dated the stay inside a tight window. Gallio is not a name Luke invented for atmosphere.

### The Delphi inscription

In 1905 French excavators at Delphi found broken pieces of an imperial letter (the Gallio / Delphi Inscription). Claudius writes about Delphi's empty citizen rolls and names **"Lucius Junius Gallio, my friend and proconsul,"** who had recently reported the problem.

The letter is dated by Claudius's titles, including his **26th imperatorial acclamation** — AD 51 / early 52. Gallio's year in Achaia is therefore **summer 51 to summer 52** (some allow spring 51). The stone confirms the office and the man. It does not photograph the hearing.

### What you can date from it

- Hearing: **51**, probably earlier in the term (Luke puts it after a long stay).
- Eighteen months in Corinth: **about 50–52**.
- Claudius's expulsion of Jews from Rome (Acts 18:2) is commonly **49**. Second, looser peg. It agrees with Gallio instead of fighting him.

From that year you count outward:

- Back: Philippi, Thessalonica, Athens, then Corinth. **1 Thessalonians** from that stay.
- Forward: Ephesus, third journey, **1 Corinthians**, painful visit, **2 Corinthians**, later winter in Corinth, **Romans**, Jerusalem, arrest.

Almost every other Pauline date is "x years after Corinth" or "y years before Corinth." That is why chronology briefs call Gallio the hard external peg.

### What it does not do

It does not date Galatians by itself. It does not decide whether Galatians 2 is Acts 11 or Acts 15. It does not settle Rome vs Ephesus for the prison letters. It assumes Luke got the *office* right — and the inscription makes that a very expensive coincidence to call a lucky guess.

### One paragraph for a group

Paul's eighteen months in Corinth sit on a Roman calendar, not only a Bible calendar. The judge in Acts 18 shows up on a letter from Claudius at Delphi in 51–52. That is why we can say 1 Thessalonians is around 50–51, and Romans is a later winter in the same city, without pretending we have Paul's birth certificate.

The bema in Corinth is still there. The fragments are in the Delphi museum. Same name as Acts 18:12.

## 2. Seneca's "letters to Paul"

Not real. Fourth-century Christian fan-fiction that grew out of the same family tree as the Gallio stone.

### Why anyone paired them

Seneca (c. 4 BC–AD 65): Nero's tutor, then advisor, then ordered to open his veins. He wrote the genuine *Moral Letters to Lucilius*. His **brother is Gallio**. That is the only hard first-century link between Paul's world and Seneca's house.

Paul and Seneca were both in Rome in the early 60s if Acts 28 is right. Both die under Nero. Tertullian later calls Seneca *saepe noster* — "often one of us" — meaning the Stoic sometimes *sounds* Christian, not that Tertullian had their mail.

Nobody in the 100s or 200s quotes a Paul–Seneca correspondence. Lactantius, who loved Seneca, still knows nothing of it in 325. Jerome (393) is the first paper trail: he includes Seneca in *On Illustrious Men* 12 *because* letters to Paul are read. Augustine mentions them in passing. Packet invented or circulating **about 325–392**. Latin style matches that century, not Nero's.

### What the packet is

Fourteen short Latin notes: eight "Seneca," six "Paul." Pretend dates 58–64. Almost empty. They flatter each other. Seneca says he has shown Paul's letters to Nero. Paul worries whose name comes first on the page. Seneca nags rustic style. One letter (often #11), different Latin and out of order, talks about the fire of Rome in 64 and Christians punished — hinting the court, not the Christians, is the problem.

No gospel. No Stoic argument. No theology worth a session object.

### Why they cannot be genuine

- Paul wrote Greek. These are Latin, and not first-century Latin.
- Seneca's real letters have a voice. These do not.
- Letter 1 already treats Paul's letters as a **bound book** — fourth-century canon habit, not AD 58.
- Some copies date letters by consuls Leo and Sabinus, a pair that does not exist on the lists.
- This "Paul" is polite and thin. Galatians and 2 Corinthians are not.
- If Jerome had found new Pauline epistles he would have shouted. He filed them as a footnote under Seneca.

Erasmus: shameless to make Seneca talk like this, blasphemy to make Paul talk like this. Renaissance humanists killed authenticity. It has stayed dead.

### Why the Middle Ages kept them

They baptized Seneca for Christian libraries. If Nero's minister had been Paul's pen-pal, pagan moral wisdom could sit on the same shelf as Romans. Copies ride in medieval Seneca manuscripts for centuries. Reception history, not history. No pagan source requires a secret conversion. No line in Paul requires a Stoic tutor in the next room.

### Real vs not real

| Real | Not real |
|---|---|
| Gallio, Seneca's brother, hears Paul in Corinth, 51–52 | Gallio introducing Paul to Seneca |
| Paul in Rome under house arrest (Acts 28) | Fourteen notes across the Palatine |
| Seneca dies 65, ordered by Nero | Seneca dying as a crypto-Christian |
| Tertullian: Seneca "often ours" in *ideas* | A conversion hidden in the letters |
| Fire of 64 and later Christian memory of Nero | Seneca's eyewitness memo to Paul |

### One paragraph for a group

The New Testament never needed a celebrity convert in Nero's cabinet. It already had a tentmaker in front of that cabinet officer's brother, and Gallio shrugged. Fourth-century Christians were embarrassed that Rome's smartest moralist never answered the mail, so they wrote the mail for him.

## 3. Chronology chairs (short)

Full chart: `PAULINE-CHRONOLOGY.md`.

**Nailed.** Gallio 51–52. Corinth stay ~50–52. 1 Thessalonians from that stay. Romans from a later Corinth winter (~56–57) with the collection. Death under Nero, mid-60s, if the early memory holds. Everything else is relative.

**Method.** Letters first (Gal 1–2 sworn) vs Acts-plus-letters vs Acts as theology-with-travel. Say which text you privilege when they collide.

**Master collision.** Gal 2:1–10 = Acts 15 council (Galatians mid-50s, usually North Galatia) or Acts 11 famine visit (Galatians ~48–49, usually South Galatia). Fourteen years counted from the road or from the first visit slides conversion.

**Corinth is a crisis sequence, not two tidy volumes.** Lost previous letter → 1 Cor from Ephesus → painful visit → tear-letter (lost, or 2 Cor 10–13) → 2 Cor from Macedonia → third visit / Romans winter.

**Prison letters.** Rome ~60–62, Caesarea ~57–59, or Ephesus ~54–56. Theology does not require settling the jail.

**Pastorals.** Release plus second arrest, or a Pauline school after his death. Do not jam 1 Timothy into Acts 19–20.

## 4. Authorship chairs (short)

Full chart: `PAULINE-AUTHORSHIP.md`.

**Seven almost uncontested.** Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon. Write as Paul.

**Swing letters.** 2 Thessalonians, Colossians, Ephesians. Early reception as Paul. Style, clock, and (for Ephesians) generic address are the doubts. Secretary/amanuensis explains some style, not automatically a new church-order.

**Pastorals.** Hardest case. Different dictionary, overseer lists, itinerary outside Acts. Defense: memos to coworkers; 2 Timothy's cloak and names. Name second-career chair and school chair once per book.

**Hebrews.** No Pauline greeting. Origen: thoughts of the apostle, phrasing of someone who wrote the teacher up; "who wrote it, God knows." Keep Hebrews its own file family.

A letter that wears Paul's name must still be *heard*. Who held the pen is a second job. Neither job cancels the other.
